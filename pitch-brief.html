How we catch a deepfake before it gets a real signature

The attack

Swisscom's onboarding asks for a photo of your ID and a selfie, then issues a legally binding electronic signature. Attackers no longer need to fool the camera. They feed a fake video straight into the app, bypassing the camera entirely. The app never sees a real person, and never sees a real camera.

Our approach

We combine several signals into one risk score: the document, the face, and the device itself. Most people pass with no extra step. Only a risky session gets an extra check. This sits on top of the service Swisscom already uses to route identity checks between providers, so it adds one more layer rather than replacing anything.

What we check

Document

Does the ID's digital signature actually match a real, unaltered document.

Face

Does the selfie match the photo on the ID.

Device

Does the background in the video move the way it would if someone were really holding the phone. A deepfake fed into the app can copy a face, but it can't make the background react to the phone's own motion.

The turn

Right at the end, we ask the person to turn their head. Which side they turn to is picked at random, in the moment, by our server.

Why we never just ask for a retry

If a check fails, we don't send the person back to try the same step again. A retry tells an attacker exactly which fake failed, so they load the next one and try again. Give them enough retries and one eventually gets through. A failed check leads to a different, harder check instead.

Why the phone-movement check

A deepfake video is generated or played back on its own. It has no way to know how someone is actually holding and tilting a phone at that exact moment. We ask the person to move the phone, then check that the picture shifts exactly the way that motion would produce. One study on selfie capture found this alone catches an attacker whose phone is sitting still on a stand or running in an emulator, since a real hand can't hold a phone perfectly still.

Why we flash red, green and blue

While the selfie is being recorded, the screen flashes a short, random sequence of pure red, green and blue, with grey pauses between them. Skin lit by the screen reflects those colours back into the camera. Pure primary colours are the easiest for a camera to tell apart, and grey pauses keep the face lit enough to still detect it and give us a stable baseline to measure against. Our server generates the sequence the moment someone starts, so a video made earlier can't have the right colours in the right order.

Real-time face-swap tools have no way to relight themselves to match a colour they don't know is coming. Researchers tested this directly against one such tool and found it simply stopped reacting to the light.

Why the turn is the strongest check

A face swap is really a flat image glued onto the video, frame by frame. It isn't a real, three-dimensional face, and that shows up in two different ways.

It can't be lit correctly from an unexpected direction. Real skin has depth, and the way it reflects light changes with the direction of that light. A flat, mapped-on texture can't reproduce that, which is why the light check catches it.

It can't turn to the side correctly either. Real-time face-swap tools track the face using flat, 2D reference points, and they lose more than half of those points once the face turns side-on. Past a certain angle, the fake visibly breaks down.

Something passing in front of the face — a hand, an ID card — breaks these tools for the same reason. They have to paint the fake face over whatever's blocking it, in real time, without any real sense of depth.

We ask for the turn last, right at the end of the selfie: it's the one moment that asks the deepfake to be something it isn't. The side is picked after the session starts, so a fake prepared in advance can't know which way to turn. This is also already standard practice: a trained human agent on a video identification call asks for exactly this. We're automating a check Swisscom's own validation specialists already trust.

The honest limit

Newer face-swap tools that build a real 3D model of the face are starting to handle a turned head better. We're not claiming this is unbeatable. We're raising the cost of the attack from a $50 tool to a research pipeline.
